Warehouse, lake, lakehouse: the vocabulary

Lesson 1 of 8. Time: 8 minutes. Tangible win: define six core terms and sort a workload into OLTP or OLAP.

The paper uses a few words very precisely. Learn them now and the rest reads easily. Everything below comes from Section 1 of the paper.

Two kinds of database workload

OLTP systems handle live transactions that touch one or a few rows at a time. The paper's example is booking a flight ticket, which adds rows to a bookings table. OLAP systems answer large analytical questions, such as the total number of bookings over a period. The paper says OLTP databases hit performance problems on that kind of question, even at small scale.

OLAP systems fit analytics because they store data in columns, their engines exploit that layout, and they usually spread work across many machines (massively parallel processing, MPP).

The paper's naming choice

"Data warehouse" has meant two things: a technology and a set of practices. The authors split them on purpose.

TermMeaning in this paper
RDBMS-OLAPThe technology: a relational database built for analytics (what most people call a data warehouse).
DW practicesTechnology-independent habits such as data modeling and data quality.
Data warehousingRDBMS-OLAP plus DW practices together.
Data lakeCheap storage that holds any kind of data in open files.
Data lakehouseAn architecture that brings warehouse capabilities to a data lake.
Data lakehousingLakehouse plus the same DW practices.
Why the split matters

Because practices are separate from technology, the paper can argue that the practices carry over unchanged to a lakehouse. Only the technology underneath changes.

Retrieval check

Which task is the best fit for an OLAP system?

Correct. Large aggregations over many rows are the OLAP pattern. The other two touch single rows, which is OLTP.

Not quite. Aggregating across many rows is OLAP. Single-row inserts and updates are OLTP.
