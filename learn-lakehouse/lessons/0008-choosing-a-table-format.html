Choosing a table format: three designs, real tradeoffs

Lesson 8 of 8. Time: 12 minutes. Tangible win: match a workload to the design tradeoff that matters and name what to test.

Lesson 5 said a table format is the enabling piece. This lesson asks how the three popular ones differ. Source: Jain et al., CIDR 2023, which compares Delta Lake, Apache Hudi, and Apache Iceberg. The authors are at UC Berkeley and Stanford, and one is at Databricks, so read it as research from people close to the field, not as a neutral audit.

Three design questions

The paper frames the differences as design choices, each forced by running on object storage that is slow and has weak transactional guarantees.

Copy-on-write versus merge-on-read

Copy-on-write (CoW) rewrites the affected files when records change. Writes cost more, reads stay fast. Merge-on-read (MoR) writes the changes to extra files and reconciles them at query time. Writes are faster, reads are slower. The paper says lower write latency can matter for workloads that apply many record-level changes, such as continuously replicating one database into another.

What the study measured

TestFinding reported by the paper
Load 3 TB of TPC-DSDelta and Iceberg took about the same time. Hudi was almost ten times slower, because it is optimized for keyed upserts and does extra pre-processing.
Run all TPC-DS queriesDelta ran 1.4 times faster than Hudi and 1.7 times faster than Iceberg overall. Per-query gaps reached 10 times.
Merge updates (CoW versus MoR)MoR merged 1.3 times faster than CoW in Hudi, with 3.2 times slower queries afterward. In Iceberg 0.14.0, MoR merged 1.4 times faster.
Plan queries on huge tablesIceberg planned small tables faster. Delta, with distributed planning, was 7 to 12 times faster on a 200K-file table.
Why you cannot just crown a winner

Everything ran on Apache Spark on AWS EMR with default settings, using Delta 2.2.0, Hudi 0.12.0, and Iceberg 1.1.0, from 2022. The paper itself traces some gaps to engine integration, such as Iceberg's custom Parquet reader and its Spark API, not only the format. It also reports Iceberg 1.1.0 merge-on-read hit S3 timeout errors, so it added results from version 0.14.0. These formats have changed since, and the paper notes Delta was planning merge-on-read support.

A tension with Lesson 6

Lesson 6 said projects like Nessie and LakeFS enable multi-table transactions. This paper says all three formats provide transactions across records in a single table, but not across tables. Both can be true: the cross-table part comes from a layer above the format.

Retrieval check

Which workload does the paper say may favor merge-on-read?

Correct. Merge-on-read writes faster at the cost of slower reads, which suits workloads applying many record-level changes.

Not quite. Copy-on-write suits read-heavy work, and most lakehouse workloads favor read speed. Merge-on-read helps when changes are frequent.

Which statement does the paper's evidence support?

Correct. The paper reports Delta and Iceberg loading in approximately the same time, with Hudi almost ten times slower.

Not quite. Hudi was slowest at bulk load, and Delta planned 7 to 12 times faster than Iceberg on the 200K-file table.

Practice

  1. Take a workload you know (for example, replicating a busy database, or append-only event logs). Say which of the three design questions matters most and why.
  2. List two reasons the study's numbers might not match your environment.
  3. Write a one-paragraph test plan using your own data. The open-source LHBench, linked from the paper, is a starting point.
